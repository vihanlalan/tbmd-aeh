\begin{abstract}
The Adaptive Markets Hypothesis (AMH) predicts that market efficiency changes with market conditions. A common way to make this testable is to detect market regimes and compare efficiency across them. We ask whether objectively detected regimes explain time variation in efficiency, and, before that, whether such regimes can be detected at all. Using daily S\&P 500 returns from 1950 to 2025, we apply Gaussian hidden Markov models (HMMs), statistical jump models and change-point detection, and evaluate every method against simulated data with and without regimes. When regimes exist, the HMM recovers them accurately (balanced accuracy 0.95 ex post and 0.91 in real time). However, the usual evidence for regimes does not separate them from volatility clustering. The Bayesian information criterion (BIC) selects four regimes in all 100 simulated GARCH(1,1)-$t$ series, which contain none. Agreement between methods on the S\&P 500 (Cohen's $\kappa$ = 0.58--0.68) is no higher than in GARCH data without regimes. A GARCH(1,1)-$t$ model also beats every HMM in sample and out of sample (Diebold--Mariano $t = -4.29$). Using state labels available in real time, daily return autocorrelation is lower in high-volatility states (difference $-0.086$, $t = -3.12$, simulation-calibrated $p = 0.01$), mainly after 2000. When the volatility level is added to the same regression, the negative regime effect disappears and changes sign, while the volatility effect remains ($t = -5.0$). Efficiency in the S\&P 500 is adaptive in the sense that it varies with volatility, but discrete regimes are not needed to describe this variation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The Efficient Market Hypothesis \cite{fama1970} treats efficiency as a fixed property of a market. The Adaptive Markets Hypothesis (AMH) of Lo \cite{lo2004, lo2017} replaces this with an evolutionary view: predictability appears and disappears as market participants, their strategies and the environment change. Empirical work supports the idea that efficiency varies over time \cite{limbrooks2011, kim2011, urquhart2013}. However, the AMH does not say which conditions matter or how to recognize them while they are happening.

A natural way to make the AMH operational is to divide history into objectively detected \emph{market regimes}, such as calm and turbulent periods, and to test whether efficiency differs between them. Regime models have a long history in finance \cite{hamilton1989, angtimmermann2012}, and newer methods such as statistical jump models \cite{nystrup2020, shu2024} have made them popular in practice. This approach rests on two assumptions that are rarely tested together. The first is that the detected regimes are real, and not an artifact of volatility clustering \cite{bollerslev1986}. It is well known that regime switching and persistent volatility are hard to tell apart \cite{lamoureux1990, hamiltonsusmel1994, gray1996, klaassen2002, diebold2001}. The second is that regime labels can be known when they are needed. Most studies use labels estimated with the full sample, which contain information from the future.

This paper asks: \emph{do objectively identified market regimes explain how market efficiency changes over time?} We answer in two steps. First, can regimes be identified objectively, and how accurately? Second, does efficiency differ across regimes when the regimes are labelled without look-ahead, and does the regime label explain anything beyond the level of volatility?

We make three contributions. First, we evaluate common regime detectors (HMMs, jump models, PELT change-point detection) on the same 76-year S\&P 500 sample. We benchmark them against simulated data in which the truth is known, including data generated by a GARCH(1,1)-$t$ process that has no regimes. This gives a null distribution for two widely used kinds of evidence: model selection by BIC and agreement between detectors. Second, we compare labels available in real time with ex-post labels across 56 years of expanding-window re-estimation. Third, we test whether return autocorrelation differs across real-time regimes. The test is calibrated by simulation and compared with a continuous measure of volatility.

The main findings are as follows. The detectors work when regimes exist. However, on the S\&P 500 neither BIC nor agreement between detectors provides evidence of regimes beyond what volatility clustering alone produces, and a no-regime GARCH model forecasts better than any HMM. Real-time labels agree only moderately with ex-post labels ($\kappa$ = 0.58--0.65). Return autocorrelation is significantly lower in real-time high-volatility states, consistent with an adaptive market. However, this effect is explained by the volatility level itself, not by the regime label. For the AMH, the conditions under which efficiency changes appear to be continuous volatility conditions rather than discrete regimes.

\section{Related Literature}

\subsection{Adaptive markets and time-varying efficiency}
Lo \cite{lo2004, lo2017} argues that efficiency depends on the population of market participants and the environment they face. Lim and Brooks \cite{limbrooks2011} survey evidence that efficiency evolves over time. Kim, Shamsuddin and Lim \cite{kim2011} find that the return predictability of the U.S. market over a century varies with market conditions. Urquhart and Hudson \cite{urquhart2013} find adaptive behavior in very long samples from major markets. These studies document variation over time but generally do not separate regimes from volatility.

\subsection{Regime identification}
Hamilton's Markov-switching model \cite{hamilton1989} is the standard parametric approach; Ang and Timmermann \cite{angtimmermann2012} review its use in finance. Statistical jump models \cite{nystrup2020, shu2024} replace the Markov chain with a penalty on state changes and are designed to produce more persistent states. Non-parametric alternatives include change-point detection, for example PELT \cite{killick2012}, and rules for dating bull and bear markets \cite{lunde2004}.

\subsection{Regimes versus volatility dynamics}
Lamoureux and Lastrapes \cite{lamoureux1990} show that shifts in the level of variance can make GARCH volatility appear more persistent. Hamilton and Susmel \cite{hamiltonsusmel1994}, Gray \cite{gray1996} and Klaassen \cite{klaassen2002} combine regime switching with ARCH/GARCH. Diebold and Inoue \cite{diebold2001} show that regime switching and long memory are easily confused, and Engle and Rangel \cite{engle2008} model slowly moving volatility. Standard likelihood-ratio tests of the number of regimes have non-standard distributions \cite{hansen1992}. All of this suggests that evidence for regimes should be judged against no-regime alternatives.

\subsection{Volatility and autocorrelation}
LeBaron \cite{lebaron1992} and Sentana and Wadhwani \cite{sentana1992} find that index return autocorrelation falls, and can turn negative, when volatility is high. Campbell, Grossman and Wang \cite{campbell1993} relate autocorrelation to trading volume. Index autocorrelation is also affected by non-synchronous trading \cite{lomackinlay1990}. We build on this evidence by asking whether a regime label adds anything to the volatility level.

\section{Data}

We use the daily closing level of the S\&P 500 index (\texttt{\textasciicircum GSPC}) from Yahoo Finance, from 3 January 1950 to 31 December 2025. This gives $n = 19{,}120$ daily log returns $r_t = 100\,\Delta \ln P_t$. For comparison we use the CBOE VIX index (from January 1990) and the NBER recession indicator (FRED series USREC). The data were downloaded on 2 October 2026.

\subsection{Regime detectors}
\emph{Gaussian HMM.} Returns follow $r_t \mid s_t = k \sim N(\mu_k, \sigma_k^2)$, with a first-order Markov chain $P(s_t = j \mid s_{t-1} = i) = a_{ij}$ \cite{hamilton1989}. We estimate $K = 2, 3, 4$ states by the Baum--Welch EM algorithm with 3--5 random restarts and order the states by volatility. Ex-post labels set state 1 (high volatility) when the smoothed probability exceeds 0.5. Real-time labels use the filtered probability instead.

\emph{Statistical jump model.} Following \cite{nystrup2020, shu2024}, states minimize
\begin{equation}
\sum_{t} \tfrac{1}{2}\,\lVert \bm{x}_t - \bm{\theta}_{s_t} \rVert^2 + \lambda \sum_{t} \mathbf{1}\{s_t \neq s_{t-1}\},
\end{equation}
where $\bm{x}_t$ contains the exponentially weighted mean return, log downside deviation and Sortino ratio at half-lives of 5, 10 and 21 days. The features are standardized on the estimation window. We use $\lambda \in \{10, 30, 100\}$, with $\lambda = 30$ as the main specification. Real-time (online) labels come from the forward pass of the dynamic program.

\emph{Change points and bear markets.} PELT \cite{killick2012} with a Gaussian mean-and-variance cost, penalty $3 \ln T$ and minimum segment length 21 days. The resulting segments are grouped into low- and high-volatility sets by $k$-means on log segment volatility. Bear markets are dated by the 20\% rule of \cite{lunde2004}.

\emph{Real-time protocol.} For each year from 1970 to 2025, the HMM and the jump model are re-estimated on all data up to the previous 31 December. Labels for the year are then produced with only past information.

\subsection{Simulation design}
We estimate four data-generating processes (DGPs) on the S\&P 500 and simulate 100 series of the same length ($T = 19{,}120$) from each:
\begin{enumerate}
\item a two-state Gaussian HMM, for which the HMM is correctly specified;
\item the same HMM with standardized $t(4)$ shocks;
\item a three-state HMM, where the truth is the top state;
\item a GARCH(1,1) model with Student-$t$ shocks \cite{bollerslev1986, bollerslev1987}, which has \emph{no regimes}.
\end{enumerate}
All detectors are applied to every series. PELT is applied to the first 25 replications per DGP because of its computational cost. Accuracy is measured by balanced accuracy and Cohen's $\kappa$ \cite{cohen1960} for the high-volatility state, the median detection delay, and the ratio of detected to true state switches.

\subsection{Is there evidence for regimes?}
\emph{BIC.} We compare $\mathrm{BIC} = -2 \ln L + p \ln T$ \cite{schwarz1978} for an i.i.d.\ Gaussian model ($K=1$) and HMMs with $K = 2, 3, 4$, where $p = (K-1) + K(K-1) + 2K$.

\emph{Regimes beyond GARCH.} We fit a GARCH(1,1)-$t$ model, map its standardized residuals to $N(0,1)$ through the fitted $t$ distribution function (a probability integral transform \cite{diebold1998}), and repeat the BIC comparison. If GARCH-$t$ is correct, the transformed residuals are i.i.d.\ normal and $K = 1$ should be selected.

\emph{Out-of-sample density forecasts.} Parameters are estimated on 1950--1999. One-step-ahead predictive log densities for 2000--2025 are compared with GARCH(1,1)-$t$ using Diebold--Mariano tests \cite{dieboldmariano1995} with Newey--West standard errors (10 lags) \cite{neweywest1987}.

\emph{Agreement against a no-regime null.} Cohen's $\kappa$ between detectors on the S\&P 500 is compared with the distribution of the same $\kappa$ in the simulated GARCH data.

\subsection{Efficiency by regime}
Let $s_{t-1} \in \{0, 1\}$ be the real-time label at $t-1$. We estimate
\begin{equation}
r_t = \alpha + \beta r_{t-1} + \gamma s_{t-1} + \delta\, s_{t-1} r_{t-1} + e_t
\label{eq:ar}
\end{equation}
with White standard errors \cite{white1980}, so that $\beta$ and $\beta + \delta$ are the first-order autocorrelations in the low- and high-volatility states. The same regression with the same real-time labelling procedure is run on every simulated series, none of which has any predictability. This gives the test's false-positive rate and simulation-calibrated $p$-values. We also compute rolling one-year Lo--MacKinlay variance-ratio tests \cite{lomackinlay1988} on real and simulated returns.

To test whether the regime label matters beyond volatility, we add $v_{t-1}$, the standardized log of a RiskMetrics exponentially weighted volatility $\hat\sigma_t^2 = 0.94\,\hat\sigma_{t-1}^2 + 0.06\, r_t^2$ known at $t-1$, and its interaction with $r_{t-1}$:
\begin{equation}
r_t = \alpha + \beta r_{t-1} + \gamma s_{t-1} + \delta\, s_{t-1} r_{t-1} + \kappa v_{t-1} + \eta\, v_{t-1} r_{t-1} + e_t .
\label{eq:race}
\end{equation}
This comparison was added after the first full run and is reported as a follow-up analysis.

\subsection{Pre-specified criteria and implementation}
Before the full run, we fixed pass/fail criteria for each question (Table~\ref{tab:score}). The HMM and jump-model computations are written in PyTorch and batched on a GPU. The forward--backward recursions and the jump-model dynamic program use parallel prefix scans. Before every run the implementation is checked against the reference packages \texttt{hmmlearn} and \texttt{jumpmodels}. On the full run (NVIDIA A100, float32), log-likelihoods matched \texttt{hmmlearn} to within 0.04, state labels agreed with $\kappa \ge 0.98$, and jump-model objectives and labels matched \texttt{jumpmodels} exactly. The full study took 27 minutes.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Q1: Do the detectors recover true regimes?}
Table~\ref{tab:sim} and Fig.~\ref{fig:sim} report accuracy in the simulations. When the HMM is correctly specified, it recovers the high-volatility state with balanced accuracy 0.951 ex post and 0.907 in real time, with a median delay of one day. With fat-tailed $t(4)$ shocks, accuracy falls to 0.862 and 0.787, and the real-time HMM switches 5.7 times as often as the true process. In the three-state process, a two-state HMM detects the crash state with $\kappa$ of only 0.25, while a three-state HMM recovers all three states ($\kappa = 0.857$). The jump models are less accurate (0.65--0.77) on all processes. This partly reflects the simulation design, which generates data from HMMs. PELT is accurate ex post (0.82--0.92) but misses 30--41\% of high-volatility episodes.

\subsection{Q2: Does the S\&P 500 have regimes beyond volatility clustering?}
\emph{Model selection.} On raw returns, BIC selects the correct number of regimes in every replication of the HMM processes. It also selects four regimes in all 100 GARCH(1,1)-$t$ series and in all 100 HMM-2 series with $t(4)$ shocks (Table~\ref{tab:bic}). BIC on raw returns therefore finds ``regimes'' whenever volatility clusters or returns are fat-tailed, as is the case for every equity index. On the S\&P 500, it also selects $K = 4$.

The GARCH-residual test controls this false-positive rate: it selects $K \ge 2$ in none of the GARCH series. However, it has almost no power, selecting $K \ge 2$ in only 1\% of the true two- and three-regime series, because the GARCH model absorbs regime-driven volatility. The S\&P 500 residuals select $K = 4$ (BIC falls from 54{,}254.5 to 53{,}870.9), a pattern that none of the four simulated processes reproduces. Because the test has no power against Markov regimes, we read this as evidence that GARCH(1,1) misses some features of the series, such as slowly moving volatility \cite{engle2008} or structural change, rather than as evidence of regimes. By our pre-specified rule the criterion is inconclusive.

Agreement between detectors is therefore not evidence of regimes.

\subsection{Q3: Can regimes be identified in real time?}
Real-time labels agree with their ex-post counterparts on 84.2\% of days for the HMM ($\kappa = 0.649$) and 80.0\% for the jump model ($\kappa = 0.576$), both below the pre-specified threshold of 0.70 (Table~\ref{tab:rt}). The real-time HMM switches state 21.5 times per year, against 4.2 times per year ex post. Much of the apparent clarity of ex-post regimes is therefore not available to an investor at the time.

\subsection{Q4: Does efficiency differ across real-time regimes?}
Table~\ref{tab:ar} reports regression~\eqref{eq:ar} with real-time labels. Over 1970--2025 the first-order autocorrelation is 0.052 ($t = 3.67$) in the HMM's low-volatility state and $-0.034$ ($t = -1.44$) in the high-volatility state. The difference is $-0.086$ ($t = -3.12$). With jump-model labels the difference is $-0.063$ ($t = -2.12$).

The test is slightly over-sized. In the simulated GARCH data without any predictability it rejects at the 5\% level in 9\% (HMM labels) and 6\% (jump labels) of replications. The 95th percentile of $|t|$ in these placebo runs is 2.10 for HMM labels and 2.05 for jump labels. Against the GARCH placebos, the calibrated $p$-values are 0.01 (HMM) and 0.04 (jump model); against all 400 simulated series they are 0.003 and 0.04.

The effect is concentrated after 2000. In 2000--2025, autocorrelation is close to zero in calm states and between $-0.11$ and $-0.12$ in turbulent states (differences: $t = -2.33$ and $-2.71$). It is not significant in 1970--1979 or 1980--1999 (Fig.~\ref{fig:eff}).

Rolling variance-ratio tests reject the random walk in 28.7\% of one-year windows over 1950--2025: 53.9\% in 1950--1979, 6.1\% in 1980--1999 and 18.3\% in 2000--2025. By contrast, the rejection rate is 4.7\%--5.3\% in simulated regime and GARCH data (95th percentile at most 7.6\%). Regime switching or volatility clustering alone therefore does not produce these rejections. The strong positive autocorrelation before 1980 (about 0.24--0.29 in the 1970s) is consistent with non-synchronous trading in the index \cite{lomackinlay1990} and should not be read as inefficiency without further evidence.

\subsection{Q5: Regime or volatility?}
Table~\ref{tab:race} and the right panel of Fig.~\ref{fig:eff} report regression~\eqref{eq:race}. On its own, the volatility interaction is stronger than either regime interaction ($t = -5.06$ over 1970--2025). It is also significant in 1970--1999 ($t = -2.54$), where neither regime label is. When both are included, the volatility interaction remains ($t = -5.00$ and $-5.45$), while the regime interaction becomes \emph{positive} ($t = 2.91$ and $3.74$). The lower autocorrelation in turbulent states is therefore explained by the volatility level. Conditional on volatility, the regime label carries no additional information in the direction the regime hypothesis predicts.

\section{Discussion}

\emph{Implications for the AMH.} The results support an adaptive view of efficiency. Return autocorrelation changes over time, and it is lower, often negative, when volatility is high, especially in the last 25 years. This fits the AMH idea that the behavior of prices depends on market conditions, and it agrees with earlier evidence on volatility and autocorrelation \cite{lebaron1992, sentana1992}. However, the results do not support describing these conditions as discrete regimes. In the S\&P 500, the regimes found by standard detectors are what volatility clustering would produce, they are hard to identify in real time, and they explain efficiency only through their link to volatility. A simpler continuous variable, the current volatility level, does the job better. For empirical work on the AMH, regime-based evidence should be checked against no-regime benchmarks and against continuous measures of market conditions.

\emph{Methodological implications.} Two kinds of evidence often presented for regimes, a BIC preference for several states and agreement between different detectors, appear in our simulations even when there are no regimes. Studies that rely on them should report a no-regime benchmark such as the one used here.

\emph{Limitations.} We study one index, at a daily frequency, with first-order autocorrelation and variance ratios as the measures of efficiency. The efficiency tests use two-state labels. Whether a three- or four-state model, for example one with a separate crash state, adds information beyond volatility is not yet tested. The GARCH-residual test has no power against Markov regimes, so it cannot rule regimes out. The regime-versus-volatility comparison was not part of the pre-specified criteria. The jump-model results depend on the feature set and penalty values, and the simulations, generated mostly from HMMs, favor the HMM. Index autocorrelation before 1980 is likely affected by non-synchronous trading. Yahoo Finance prices may be revised over time.

\section{Conclusion}
We asked whether objectively identified market regimes explain how efficiency changes over time in the S\&P 500 from 1950 to 2025. Regime detectors work when regimes exist, but the usual evidence for regimes in the S\&P 500 is matched by volatility clustering alone, a no-regime GARCH model fits and forecasts better, and real-time labels differ substantially from ex-post ones. Market efficiency, measured by return autocorrelation with labels available in real time, is lower in turbulent periods. This variation is explained by the level of volatility, not by the regime label. The market is adaptive, but in this sample it adapts to volatility rather than switching between regimes.
